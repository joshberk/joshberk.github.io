Demystifying Advanced Persistent Threats

April 2023 • Research Reflection

During a team project at the University of Cincinnati in early 2023, I had the opportunity to dive deeply into the murky world of advanced persistent threats (APTs). APTs are highly coordinated attacks undertaken by skilled adversaries who methodically compromise organisations over extended periods. Rather than smash‑and‑grab intrusions, these campaigns unfold slowly, adapting to their targets’ defences and objectives.

Our research began by cataloguing the tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs) commonly employed by APT groups. We examined publicly available incident reports, analysed malware samples and mapped each behaviour to the MITRE ATT&CK framework. This exercise revealed patterns: initial access often exploited spear‑phishing or supply‑chain compromises, lateral movement leveraged stolen credentials or living‑off‑the‑land tools, and exfiltration used encrypted channels to avoid detection.

Understanding TTPs, however, was only the first step. To proactively defend against APTs, we integrated behavioural analytics into our threat detection models. Instead of searching for specific malware signatures, we looked for deviations in network flow, unusual user behaviour and timing anomalies that might indicate an adversary’s presence. By correlating these subtle signals, our prototype was capable of flagging suspicious activity earlier in the attack chain.

The project underscored that combating APTs requires patience, curiosity and collaboration. Attackers are constantly evolving, so our defences must evolve too. Continual sharing of threat intelligence, combined with rigorous research and practical experimentation, is key to staying a step ahead.
